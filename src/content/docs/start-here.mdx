## Welcome

This course takes you from zero to writing scripts that build and check chip layouts. You need no programming and no science background: if you can do arithmetic and read an (x, y) point on a graph, you are ready.

- **40 weeks, about 3 hours a week.** Free, with no sign-up.
- **Nothing to install for the first 10 weeks.** All code runs in your browser.
- **No paid software, ever.** You will use free tools only.

## The big picture

The course has four milestones. Each one ends with a project, called a capstone, and each capstone reuses the one before.

| Milestone | Weeks | You learn | Your code runs in | Capstone |

## How a lesson page works

Every lesson uses the same sections, in the same order, so you always know where you are:

1. **Chip story**: why today's idea matters for Lumen-1.
2. **You will be able to…**: at most three goals.
3. **Warm-up**: three questions from earlier lessons.
4. **Concept and worked example**: a short explanation, then fully explained code. These two can repeat for each new idea.
5. **Faded example**: the same kind of code with a few blanks for you to fill in.
6. **Predict → Run → Explain**: you write down what the code will print before you run it.
7. **Common traps**: real error messages and how to fix them.
8. **Exit quiz**, **cheat card** and **further reading**.

## Why this course works

The format is based on research about how people learn. It may feel harder than reading, and that is on purpose.

**Quizzing beats rereading.** Pulling an answer out of your memory makes the memory stronger. In a large review of study methods, Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing as one of the two most useful techniques. Rereading and highlighting were rated much lower. That is why every lesson starts and ends with questions, and why every answer comes with an explanation.

**Spacing beats cramming.** The other top technique in the same review is spreading practice out over time. So the warm-ups bring back earlier weeks, the weekly quiz mixes old and new, and your review cards return after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days.

**Worked examples first, then less help.** Beginners learn faster by studying a fully solved example than by struggling alone (the worked-example effect, from John Sweller's research). But as you improve, too much help starts to get in the way (Kalyuga and colleagues). So the help fades: Weeks 1–10 give two worked examples per lesson, M20 gives one plus a broken script to fix, and by M30 you get a short spec and tests.

**Predict before you run.** Writing your prediction first is a small self-test. If you are wrong, you see it at once, together with the reason. Errors are not failures here. They are the fastest way to find a wrong idea and fix it.

### How to study each week

- [ ] Do the warm-up from memory, before you look anything up.
- [ ] Type every prediction before you press Run.
- [ ] When an error appears, read the message first. It usually names the problem.
- [ ] Run the lab check until you see `RESULT: PASS`.
- [ ] Take the weekly quiz a day or two after the lessons, without notes.
- [ ] Spend 5 minutes on your [spaced review](/SJTU-VLSI-TCL-Language/review/) cards on two other days.
- [ ] Before a new week, open its "Review these first" links.
